# EQUATOR Network: Finding and Using the Right Reporting Guideline

> **Kuhn knowledge card.** Canonical source: https://www.equator-network.org/ (EQUATOR Network — Enhancing the QUAlity and Transparency Of health Research). Source access/license: free, open access; individual guidelines carry their own (usually open) licenses. This card is a Kuhn-authored summary — cite and consult the canonical source for authoritative text.

## Scope

The EQUATOR Network is the central library and clearinghouse for health-research
reporting guidelines — over 250 of them — plus training materials and toolkits
for authors, editors, and peer reviewers. It does not itself prescribe how to
report a study; it indexes the guidelines that do, matched to study design.
Relevant whenever you are drafting or reviewing a manuscript describing human
health research: trials, observational studies, systematic reviews, diagnostic
accuracy studies, qualitative research, case reports, protocols, prediction
models, economic evaluations, and more. Many principles transfer to non-health
scientific writing, since the underlying goal is complete, transparent, and
reproducible reporting.

## Key requirements

The core discipline EQUATOR promotes: **identify your study design, then report
against the matching guideline's checklist.** The main mappings to know:

- **Randomized controlled trials → CONSORT** (checklist + participant flow
  diagram); trial protocols → SPIRIT. CONSORT 2025 is the current version.
- **Observational studies (cohort, case-control, cross-sectional) → STROBE**
  (22-item checklist keyed to manuscript sections).
- **Systematic reviews and meta-analyses → PRISMA 2020** (checklist + study
  selection flow diagram); protocols → PRISMA-P.
- **Diagnostic/prognostic accuracy studies → STARD**; prediction models →
  TRIPOD (TRIPOD+AI for machine-learning models).
- **Qualitative research → SRQR or COREQ.**
- **Case reports → CARE.** Quality improvement → SQUIRE. Animal (preclinical)
  studies → ARRIVE. Study protocols, economic evaluations (CHEERS), survey
  research, and many niche designs have their own entries.

Each guideline typically ships three artifacts: a **checklist** (numbered items
mapped to title, abstract, introduction, methods, results, discussion, and
other-information sections), a **flow diagram** where participant or record
flow matters, and an **explanation-and-elaboration (E&E) paper** giving the
rationale and worked examples for every item. Extensions adapt base guidelines
to variants (cluster trials, abstracts, equity-focused reviews, AI
interventions such as CONSORT-AI/SPIRIT-AI).

Using a guideline means more than citing it: authors are expected to complete
the checklist, indicate the page or section where each item is addressed, and
submit it with the manuscript — many journals require this. Reporting
guidelines constrain *reporting*, not design; they tell you what to disclose
about what you did, including limitations, not how to run the study.

## How to apply when writing

- **Before drafting**, search the EQUATOR library (browse by study type or use
  the search/wizard tools) to select the guideline and any relevant extensions.
  If several could apply, use the most specific one and note companions.
- **Structure the methods and results around the checklist.** Treat each item
  as a prompt: eligibility criteria, setting and dates, variables and
  measurement, bias handling, sample-size rationale, statistical methods,
  missing-data handling, participant flow with reasons for exclusion, and
  numbers behind every percentage.
- **Draft the flow diagram early** (CONSORT/PRISMA) — assembling counts late in
  writing is a common source of internal inconsistency.
- **State the guideline in the manuscript** (commonly in Methods: "reported in
  accordance with STROBE") and cite it; attach the completed checklist as
  supplementary material with page references.
- **Use the E&E paper** whenever a checklist item is ambiguous — it contains
  published examples of adequate reporting for that item.
- For protocols and registered reports, apply the protocol-stage guideline
  (SPIRIT, PRISMA-P) so the eventual results paper maps cleanly onto it.

## Common pitfalls

- Citing a reporting guideline as if it validated study quality — checklists
  certify complete reporting, not sound design.
- Picking the wrong guideline for the design (e.g., using CONSORT for a
  non-randomized comparison instead of STROBE or TREND).
- Completing the checklist retrospectively as a submission formality, leaving
  items genuinely unreported in the text.
- Ignoring applicable extensions (cluster designs, crossover trials, abstracts,
  AI interventions) that add required items.
- Flow-diagram counts that do not reconcile with the numbers in tables and
  text.
- Using an outdated version (e.g., PRISMA 2009 instead of PRISMA 2020, or
  pre-2025 CONSORT) when journals expect the current one.
