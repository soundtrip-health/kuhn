# ARRIVE 2.0: Reporting Guidelines for Animal Research

> **Kuhn knowledge card.** Canonical source: https://www.arriveguidelines.org/ (ARRIVE Guidelines Collaborative / NC3Rs; published open access in PLOS Biology, 2020). Source access/license: open access (CC BY publication); checklist and guidance freely downloadable. This card is a Kuhn-authored summary — cite and consult the canonical source for authoritative text.

## Scope

ARRIVE 2.0 (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments) governs the
reporting of any study involving live animals — preclinical pharmacology,
toxicology, disease models, behavioral and physiological research — in journal
articles, theses, and grant reports. It replaced the 2010 original, splitting
the guidance into a prioritized "Essential 10" (the minimum a reader needs to
appraise and reproduce the work) and a "Recommended Set" (context that
completes the record). Endorsed by major journals and funders; many require a
completed ARRIVE checklist at submission. It concerns reporting, not ethics
approval or experimental design per se — though reporting honestly against it
quickly exposes design weaknesses.

## Key requirements

**The Essential 10** (paraphrased — consult the source for exact wording):

1. **Study design** — describe the design elements: groups compared, the
   experimental unit (animal, litter, cage), and how the study was organized.
2. **Sample size** — exact numbers per group, the total, and how the size was
   decided (a priori power calculation or other rationale).
3. **Inclusion/exclusion criteria** — criteria set in advance; any animals or
   data points excluded, with reasons; exact n analyzed per group per outcome.
4. **Randomization** — whether and how animals were allocated to groups
   (method of sequence generation), plus strategies for controlling confounders
   such as cage position or treatment order.
5. **Blinding (masking)** — who was unaware of group assignment at each stage:
   allocation, conduct, outcome assessment, analysis.
6. **Outcome measures** — all outcomes assessed, with the primary outcome
   identified where the study tests a hypothesis.
7. **Statistical methods** — the analysis for each outcome, the experimental
   unit used in analysis, and how assumptions were checked.
8. **Experimental animals** — species, strain and substrain, sex, age or
   weight, and source; genetic modification status where relevant.
9. **Experimental procedures** — what was done, when, how, and why: doses,
   routes, anesthesia/analgesia, equipment — in enough detail to repeat.
10. **Results** — for each experiment, summary statistics with a measure of
    variability, and effect sizes with confidence intervals where applicable.

**The Recommended Set** adds: abstract content; background and rationale
(including why animals and this model); objectives; ethics statement (approval
body, license/protocol number); housing and husbandry conditions; animal care
and monitoring (welfare assessment, humane endpoints, adverse events);
interpretation limitations and implications for replacement, reduction, and
refinement (the 3Rs); generalizability/translation to other species including
humans; protocol registration; data access; and declarations of funding and
conflicts of interest.

## How to apply when writing

- Draft Methods with the Essential 10 as your outline — design, animals,
  procedures, sample size, randomization, blinding, outcomes, statistics all
  need an explicit sentence, even when the answer is "not done" (say so rather
  than staying silent).
- Name the experimental unit and be consistent: if littermates share a cage
  and treatment, the cage may be the unit — the n and the statistics must
  match it.
- Report animals as they actually were: "male C57BL/6J mice, 8–10 weeks,
  22–26 g, Jackson Laboratory" — not just "mice."
- Track attrition per group and reconcile: animals allocated, excluded (with
  reasons), and analyzed for each outcome; consider a flow diagram for complex
  designs.
- In Results, give exact group sizes with every figure and table, plot
  individual data points where feasible, and prefer effect sizes with 95%
  confidence intervals over bare p-values.
- In Discussion, address limitations of the model and translation cautiously;
  include the ethics approval statement and complete the journal's ARRIVE
  checklist with section references before submission.

## Common pitfalls

- Reporting sample size without the rationale behind it, or an n that silently
  changes between figures because exclusions went unreported.
- Claiming randomization or blinding without describing the method — "animals
  were randomized" alone is not compliant.
- Pseudoreplication: analyzing repeated measures or cage-mates as independent
  animals, inflating the effective n.
- Post hoc exclusion of "outlier" animals with no pre-specified criteria.
- Omitting sex, age, or supplier of animals, making replication impossible and
  masking sex-specific effects.
- Describing husbandry and welfare only in the ethics paperwork, leaving the
  published record silent on conditions that materially affect results.
