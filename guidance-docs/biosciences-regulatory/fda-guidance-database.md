# Finding and Citing FDA Guidance Documents

> **Kuhn knowledge card.** Canonical source: https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents (US Food and Drug Administration). Source access/license: US government works, public domain — freely reproducible. This card is a Kuhn-authored summary — cite and consult the canonical source for authoritative text.

## Scope

FDA guidance documents state the agency's *current thinking* on regulatory
topics — study design, submission content, manufacturing, labeling, statistics
— across drugs (CDER), biologics (CBER), devices (CDRH), foods, and other
regulated products. This card covers how to locate the right guidance, read
its status correctly, and cite it in regulatory and scientific documents. Key
legal frame: under FDA's Good Guidance Practices (21 CFR 10.115), guidance is
**not binding** on the agency or the public — it describes a recommended,
generally acceptable approach, and alternative approaches are permitted if
they satisfy the underlying statutes and regulations. Requirements live in the
Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, the Public Health Service Act, and the
Code of Federal Regulations; guidance explains how to meet them.

## Key requirements

**The database.** The searchable portal at the canonical URL covers guidance
from all FDA centers. Filterable by product area (drugs, biologics, medical
devices…), issuing center/office, status (draft vs final), open comment
period, and issue date; full-text search covers titles and summaries. Centers
also maintain topical portals (e.g., CDER's guidance pages organized by
development phase and topic).

**Draft vs final status — always check.** A *draft* guidance is distributed
for comment (with a docket number at Regulations.gov) and represents proposed
thinking; a *final* guidance represents current thinking. Drafts are routinely
followed in practice but must be identified as drafts when cited, typically
with the label "Draft Guidance — Not for implementation" on the document
itself. A new revision (indicated as "Revision 1/2…" or by a superseding
issue) replaces earlier versions; some ICH-derived guidances carry revision
codes like E6(R3). Withdrawn guidances remain findable but no longer reflect
agency thinking.

**Naming conventions.** Most are styled "Guidance for Industry" (others:
"Guidance for FDA Staff," "Guidance for Industry and FDA Staff," or
review-staff guidances). ICH guidelines adopted by FDA are republished as FDA
guidances under their ICH codes (e.g., "E3 Structure and Content of Clinical
Study Reports"). Titles are the stable identifier — FDA guidances have no
numbering system like ICH's, so cite by title, center, status, and date.

**A workable citation pattern** (adapt to house style):
*US Food and Drug Administration. Title of the Guidance. Guidance for
Industry. [Draft, if applicable.] Center (e.g., CDER/CBER). Month Year. URL
(guidance landing page). Accessed date.* Prefer the HTML landing page over the
`fda.gov/media/NNNNN/download` PDF link — media IDs can change when documents
are revised; the landing page persists and always points to the current file.

## How to apply when writing

- **Search before drafting** any regulatory section: filter the database by
  product type and topic to find product-specific guidance (e.g., a disease-
  area development guidance) plus the cross-cutting ones (CMC, clinical
  pharmacology, statistical).
- **Record status and date at first citation** — "FDA draft guidance (Month
  Year)" vs final — and re-verify before submission; a draft may have
  finalized, or a final guidance may have been revised, between drafting and
  filing.
- **Frame recommendations correctly**: write "consistent with FDA's guidance
  on X, the study used…" rather than "as required by FDA guidance" — guidance
  recommends; regulations require. Cite the CFR section when something truly
  is a requirement.
- **Justify deviations**: where your approach departs from a relevant
  guidance, acknowledge the guidance and give the scientific rationale for the
  alternative approach — silence invites information requests.
- **For ICH-derived topics**, cite the FDA-adopted guidance in FDA-facing
  documents (and the ICH original where an international audience matters);
  the technical content is harmonized.
- Because FDA guidance is public domain, quoting exact recommendation language
  is permissible — but attribute it and quote the current version.

## Common pitfalls

- Citing a superseded or withdrawn guidance because a stale PDF link still
  resolves — always confirm currency in the database.
- Presenting a draft guidance as final, or omitting "draft" from the citation.
- Deep-linking `fda.gov/media/…/download` URLs that break or silently point to
  an old revision after an update.
- Calling guidance recommendations "requirements," or conversely dismissing a
  guidance as "only guidance" without offering a justified alternative — the
  reviewer will still expect the underlying issue addressed.
- Missing product-specific guidance because the search used only generic terms
  — browse the relevant center's topical listing too.
- Confusing FDA guidance with the CFR, compliance policy guides, or MAPPs/
  SOPPs (internal agency procedures), which have different weight and cite
  differently.
