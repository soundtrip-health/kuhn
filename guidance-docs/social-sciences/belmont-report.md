# The Belmont Report

Source access/license: U.S. government work, public domain; freely accessible (also archived at https://archive.org/details/belmontreporteth00unit). ## Scope

The Belmont Report (1978, published 1979) is the foundational ethical framework for all research involving human subjects in the United States. It was written by the National Commission created under the National Research Act of 1974, partly in response to abuses such as the Tuskegee syphilis study.

It applies to biomedical and behavioral research alike — surveys, interviews, experiments, interventions, secondary analysis of identifiable data — and underlies the Common Rule (45 CFR 46) and IRB review everywhere in U.S. research. Any manuscript reporting human-subjects research (social science, psychology, health, education) is expected to demonstrate compliance with its principles, usually via an ethics/IRB statement and a description of consent procedures.

The Report also draws a working boundary between *practice* (interventions intended solely to benefit an individual patient or client) and *research* (activities designed to develop or contribute to generalizable knowledge). Activities with any research component should undergo ethical review.

**Relationship to regulation.** The Belmont Report itself is a statement of principles, not a regulation; its requirements are made binding by the Common Rule (45 CFR 46) and enforced through IRB review. When a manuscript cites "Belmont principles," reviewers read that as shorthand for the consent, risk/benefit, and selection standards summarized below. Internationally, the Declaration of Helsinki and CIOMS guidelines play the analogous role; multi-country studies typically cite both frameworks.

## Key requirements

The Report rests on three basic ethical principles, each paired with a practical application.

**1. Respect for persons → informed consent.** Individuals are autonomous agents whose choices must be honored, and people with diminished autonomy (children, prisoners, cognitively impaired persons) are entitled to additional protection. The application is informed consent, analyzed as three elements:

- *Information* — purpose of the research, procedures, risks, anticipated benefits, alternatives, and an explicit invitation to ask questions and to withdraw at any time. The standard is what a "reasonable volunteer" would want to know; incomplete disclosure is justifiable only when truly necessary, when withheld risks are minimal, and when debriefing follows.
- *Comprehension* — information must be conveyed in a manner and context the subject can actually understand (language, literacy, timing); capacity to understand must be considered.
- *Voluntariness* — consent is valid only if free of coercion (threat of harm) and undue influence (excessive or inappropriate reward), with particular care where subjects are in dependent or vulnerable positions.

**2. Beneficence → assessment of risks and benefits.** Two complementary duties: do not harm, and maximize possible benefits while minimizing possible harms. The application is a systematic risk/benefit assessment:

- Risks span physical, psychological, legal, social, and economic harm, and must be judged by both probability and magnitude.
- Risks must be justified by anticipated benefits to subjects or by the importance of the knowledge to be gained; brutal or inhumane treatment is never justified.
- Risks should be reduced to those strictly necessary to the research design.
- When vulnerable populations are involved, the appropriateness of involving them at all must itself be demonstrated.

**3. Justice → fair selection of subjects.** The burdens and benefits of research must be distributed fairly:

- *Individual level* — not offering risky research only to disfavored persons, or potentially beneficial research only to favored ones.
- *Social level* — not drawing subjects from convenient, institutionalized, or dependent populations merely because they are easy to recruit (e.g., welfare recipients, prisoners, patients in public wards).
- Populations that bear the burdens of research should stand to share in its benefits.

## How to apply when writing

- **Methods — ethics statement.** State the reviewing IRB/ethics committee, approval or exemption number, and that the study conformed to applicable regulations (Common Rule) and the principles of the Belmont Report. Place it early in Methods, typically under Participants or Procedure.
- **Methods — consent.** Describe *how* consent was obtained (written, oral, electronic), what participants were told, and how comprehension and voluntariness were supported. For minors or adults with diminished capacity, describe assent plus parent/guardian permission or legally authorized representative consent.
- **Waivers and deception.** If consent was waived or disclosure was incomplete (deception designs), state the IRB's justification and the debriefing procedure.
- **Methods — recruitment and selection.** Justify the sampling frame in terms of the research question, not convenience alone. If the study enrolls a vulnerable or historically over-researched population, explain why that population is scientifically necessary and what extra protections applied.
- **Methods/Discussion — risk and benefit.** Describe risk-minimization measures (confidentiality protections, data de-identification, distress protocols, compensation that is fair but not coercive). In Discussion, frame benefits honestly — do not overstate direct benefit to participants when the real benefit is generalizable knowledge.
- **Data availability.** Reconcile open-science commitments with confidentiality promises made at consent; describe de-identification or controlled access rather than claiming data cannot be shared at all.

## Common pitfalls

- A bare "IRB approval was obtained" with no consent description — reviewers increasingly expect the consent process, not just the approval number.
- Treating consent as a signed form rather than a process; ignoring comprehension (e.g., dense forms for low-literacy populations).
- Recruiting from captive or convenient populations (students, patients, online panels in low-income countries) without justifying selection under the justice principle.
- Describing deception or incomplete disclosure without stating the debriefing procedure and IRB justification.
- Overstating direct participant benefit, or omitting non-physical risks (privacy, legal, social stigma) from the risk discussion.
- Promising confidentiality at consent and later publishing re-identifiable detail (small subgroups, verbatim quotes with identifying context).
