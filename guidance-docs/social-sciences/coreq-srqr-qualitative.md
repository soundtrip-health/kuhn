# Qualitative Research Reporting: COREQ and SRQR

> **Kuhn knowledge card.** Canonical sources: COREQ — https://www.equator-network.org/reporting-guidelines/coreq/ (EQUATOR Network; Tong, Sainsbury & Craig 2007); SRQR — https://www.equator-network.org/reporting-guidelines-study-design/qualitative-research/ (EQUATOR Network; O'Brien et al. 2014). ## Scope

Two complementary reporting guidelines for qualitative studies:

- **COREQ** (COnsolidated criteria for REporting Qualitative research) is a 32-item checklist aimed specifically at **interview and focus-group studies** — the dominant qualitative designs in health and social science. It is organized into three domains: (1) research team and reflexivity, (2) study design, (3) analysis and findings.
- **SRQR** (Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research) is a 21-item standard applicable to **any qualitative design** — grounded theory, phenomenology, ethnography, case study, discourse analysis, thematic analysis — spanning the whole manuscript from title to conflicts of interest.

Use COREQ when the study is interview/focus-group based (many journals require its checklist at submission); use SRQR for other qualitative designs or when a broader, design-agnostic standard fits better. They overlap heavily; satisfying both is mostly a matter of covering the domains below.

Related standards to know about: ENTREQ (synthesis of qualitative research), JARS-Qual (APA journals), and the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) for appraising qualitative components in reviews. Neither COREQ nor SRQR is a quality-appraisal instrument — they specify what must be *reported*, not how the study should have been conducted.

## Key requirements

**Research team and reflexivity (COREQ Domain 1; SRQR "researcher characteristics").** Report who collected the data and what they brought to it:

- Interviewer/facilitator identity, credentials, occupation, gender, training, and experience in qualitative methods.
- The relationship between researchers and participants — whether one existed before the study, and what participants knew about the interviewer and their goals.
- The assumptions, interests, and standpoint the researchers brought. In qualitative work the researcher is the instrument, so readers must be able to judge how researcher characteristics could shape data and interpretation.

**Study design (COREQ Domain 2; SRQR problem/approach/methods items).** Report:

- *Theoretical framing:* the methodological orientation underpinning the study (e.g., grounded theory, phenomenology, content analysis) and, for SRQR, the guiding theory or paradigm and the study's purpose/question.
- *Sampling:* how participants were selected (purposive, convenience, snowball, theoretical), how they were approached, how many participated, and how many declined or dropped out and why.
- *Setting and context:* where data were collected, who else was present, and relevant sample characteristics.
- *Data collection:* whether an interview guide was used and piloted; whether interviews were repeated; whether sessions were audio/video recorded; whether field notes were taken; session duration; whether and why data collection stopped (e.g., saturation); whether transcripts were returned to participants for checking.

**Analysis and findings (COREQ Domain 3; SRQR analysis, results, and integrity items).** Report:

- The number of coders and how the coding tree or category system was developed.
- Whether themes were derived in advance or from the data; any software used; whether participants gave feedback on findings.
- **Trustworthiness/integrity** (explicit in SRQR): the techniques used to enhance rigor — triangulation, member checking, audit trail, peer debriefing, negative-case analysis — and acknowledgment of the researchers' influence on the analysis.
- Grounded findings: themes clearly presented, illustrated with identified (but anonymized) participant quotations, consistency between data and interpretation, and inclusion of diverse or discrepant cases — not just confirming ones.

## How to apply when writing

- **Methods:** open with the methodological orientation and the rationale for a qualitative approach; then sampling strategy and recruitment numbers (including refusals); setting; data-collection procedures (guide, recording, duration, stopping rule); and the analysis process step by step — who coded, how codes became themes, what software, what integrity checks.
- **Add a reflexivity/positionality paragraph** — team composition, relevant experience, prior relationships with participants, and how the team's standpoint was managed (bracketing, memoing, debriefing).
- **Results/Findings:** organize by theme; for each theme give an interpretive claim plus one or two short anonymized quotations tagged with a participant identifier (e.g., "P07, nurse"); report discrepant cases and how they were handled.
- **Discussion:** connect findings to the framing theory or literature; state transferability limits rather than statistical generalizability.
- **Submission:** complete the COREQ or SRQR checklist with page numbers; many journals require it as a supplementary file.
- **Choosing between them:** interview or focus-group study → COREQ; ethnography, document analysis, observation-based, or multi-method qualitative design → SRQR.

## Common pitfalls

- No reflexivity reporting at all — the most commonly missed COREQ domain.
- "Interviews were conducted until saturation" with no account of how saturation was assessed or at what point.
- Reporting only the number interviewed, omitting how many were approached and declined.
- Themes presented without supporting quotations, or quotations without participant identifiers, making grounding unverifiable.
- Describing analysis as "thematic analysis" with no detail on coder count, codebook development, or reconciliation of disagreements.
- Quantifying qualitative findings ("60% of participants felt…") as if frequencies from purposive samples were estimates.
