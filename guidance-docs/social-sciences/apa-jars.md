# APA Journal Article Reporting Standards (JARS)

> **Kuhn knowledge card.** Canonical source: https://apastyle.apa.org/jars (American Psychological Association). Source access/license: freely accessible web resource; the underlying tables and articles are APA-copyrighted — this card paraphrases, it does not reproduce them. This card is a Kuhn-authored summary — cite and consult the canonical source for authoritative text.

## Scope

JARS defines what information must appear in an empirical research article submitted to APA-style journals — psychology, education, and much of the behavioral and social sciences. It is organized into parallel standards families:

- **JARS-Quant** — quantitative studies: experiments (with and without random assignment), observational/correlational designs, longitudinal studies, replications, N-of-1 designs, and meta-analyses (MARS).
- **JARS-Qual** — qualitative studies: interviews, ethnography, narrative, grounded theory, and related designs, plus qualitative meta-analyses.
- **JARS-Mixed** — mixed-methods studies, combining both families.
- **JARS-REC** — race, ethnicity, and culture: guidance for describing samples and framing findings in culturally sensitive, bias-free terms across all study types.

JARS governs *reporting completeness*, not methodology itself: it tells authors what readers need in order to evaluate and potentially replicate the work.

## Key requirements

**Common structure (all families).** Title and abstract that identify the study design; introduction stating the problem, its importance, prior evidence, and the specific aims or hypotheses (and which analyses were planned vs. exploratory); methods, results/findings, and discussion sections with the detail below; funding, conflicts of interest, and data/registration statements.

**JARS-Quant essentials, paraphrased:**

- *Participants:* eligibility criteria, demographic characteristics, sampling procedure, settings and dates of data collection, how sample size was determined (power analysis or other rationale), and agreements/payments made to participants.
- *Participant flow:* numbers approached, screened, enrolled, lost, and analyzed at each stage — for experiments, per condition, ideally with a flow diagram; reasons for attrition and how missing data were handled.
- *Measures:* every instrument with evidence of score reliability and validity in this sample.
- *Procedures:* exact procedures in replicable detail; masking/blinding where relevant; for manipulations, fidelity or manipulation checks.
- *Design and assignment:* for randomized designs, the method of random assignment and concealment; for nonrandomized designs, how groups were formed and what confounding controls were applied.
- *Analysis and results:* the statistical model for each hypothesis; exact p values, effect sizes, and confidence intervals rather than significance verdicts alone; assumption checks; a clear separation of preregistered/confirmatory analyses from exploratory ones; all outcomes analyzed, not just significant ones.

**JARS-Qual essentials, paraphrased:**

- The researchers' own perspectives and relationship to participants (reflexivity).
- The methodological approach and the research tradition it belongs to.
- Recruitment and participant-selection rationale, and how data collection proceeded and evolved.
- The analysis process: who coded, and how categories or themes were developed.
- Methodological-integrity evidence: grounding in data, saturation or adequacy rationale, triangulation, audit trails, member checking where used.
- Findings presented with sufficient excerpts/evidence to show how interpretations arise from data — without reducing qualitative findings to counts.

**JARS-Mixed essentials, paraphrased:** state the rationale for mixing, the mixed-methods design type, the timing and priority of strands, and how strands were integrated (merging, connecting, embedding); report each strand to its own family's standard.

## How to apply when writing

- Choose the applicable JARS table(s) before drafting; use them as a Methods/Results outline rather than a post-hoc checklist.
- Report sample-size determination and analytic plan as decided *a priori*; label anything decided after seeing data as exploratory.
- Pair every inferential test with an effect size and interval estimate; report nulls with the same completeness as significant results.
- Include a participant-flow account (numbers and reasons) whenever there is any attrition, exclusion, or screening.
- For qualitative work, write a reflexivity/positionality passage and describe the analysis chain from raw data to themes.
- Apply JARS-REC when describing samples: report how race/ethnicity/culture categories were assessed, use participant-preferred terminology, and avoid treating any group as the unmarked default.
- In the abstract, name the design (e.g., "randomized experiment," "grounded-theory interview study") — JARS starts at the title and abstract, not at Methods.

## Common pitfalls

- Stating "a power analysis was conducted" without the effect size assumption, alpha, power, and resulting N.
- Reporting only the instruments' published reliabilities, not reliability in the current sample.
- Silent dropping of participants, items, conditions, or outcomes between design and results.
- Presenting exploratory findings with confirmatory language ("as hypothesized") when no hypothesis was preregistered.
- Qualitative reports that list themes with no reflexivity statement and no account of how themes were derived.
- Mixed-methods papers that report two parallel studies but never describe the integration.
