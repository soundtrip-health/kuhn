# IPCC Calibrated Uncertainty Language

> **Kuhn knowledge card.** Canonical source: https://archive.ipcc.ch/pdf/supporting-material/uncertainty-guidance-note.pdf (IPCC — Guidance Note for Lead Authors of the IPCC Fifth Assessment Report on Consistent Treatment of Uncertainties, Mastrandrea et al. 2010). Source access/license: freely accessible public IPCC supporting material. This card is a Kuhn-authored summary — cite and consult the canonical source for authoritative text.

## Scope

The AR5 uncertainty guidance note defines the calibrated language the IPCC uses to communicate how certain a finding is. It has become the reference vocabulary for uncertainty communication in climate and environmental assessment writing generally — assessment reports, syntheses, environmental impact analyses, and any document where expert judgment must be conveyed with graded confidence.

It provides two coupled metrics: a qualitative **confidence** scale grounded in evidence and agreement, and a quantitative **likelihood** scale mapped to probability ranges. The AR6 risk guidance note (2021) builds on the same calibration for hazard/exposure/vulnerability framing.

## Key requirements

**Evidence and agreement.** Every key finding is first characterized on two dimensions, which can be stated directly (e.g., "medium evidence, high agreement"):

- *Evidence* — limited, medium, or robust, judged by the amount, quality, consistency, and type of evidence (data, models, theory, expert judgment).
- *Agreement* — low, medium, or high, across lines of evidence and among experts.

**Confidence scale (qualitative).** Evidence and agreement combine into a confidence level on a five-step scale: **very low, low, medium, high, very high.**

- Confidence rises with both dimensions: robust evidence plus high agreement supports high or very high confidence; limited evidence or low agreement caps it.
- Confidence is a judgment about the *validity of a finding*, not a probability.
- Convention: italicized in parentheses after the finding — "(high confidence)."

**Likelihood scale (quantitative).** When the uncertainty can be quantified (from observations, models, or formal expert elicitation), findings use calibrated likelihood terms tied to probability ranges of the outcome:

- *virtually certain* — 99–100%
- *extremely likely* — 95–100%
- *very likely* — 90–100%
- *likely* — 66–100%
- *about as likely as not* — 33–66%
- *unlikely* — 0–33%
- *very unlikely* — 0–10%
- *extremely unlikely* — 0–5%
- *exceptionally unlikely* — 0–1%

These are reserved meanings: in calibrated text, "likely" is a claim of at least two-in-three probability, not a casual hedge. A likelihood statement is itself made with an (often implicit high or very high) confidence level; likelihood should not be assigned when confidence in the underlying science is low.

**Traceability and calibration discipline.** Calibrated statements must have a "traceable account" — the text should let a reader find the evidence and reasoning behind each confidence or likelihood assignment. Author teams are told to consider the full range of plausible outcomes (guarding against anchoring and overconfidence), to state what is known and unknown, and to avoid false precision — while equally avoiding vagueness when quantification is possible.

## How to apply when writing

- **Choose the right register per finding.** Qualitative understanding → evidence/agreement or confidence language; quantified outcome probabilities → likelihood terms; well-characterized distributions → give the numbers (ranges, percentiles) directly.
- **Attach the qualifier to the finding, consistently formatted:** "Summer low flows have declined across the basin *(high confidence)*"; "Under the high-emissions scenario, exceedance of the 2 °C threshold by 2050 is *very likely*."
- **Reserve the calibrated words.** In a document using this scheme, do not use "likely," "unlikely," or "virtually certain" colloquially; pick uncalibrated synonyms (plausible, expected, conceivable) for informal uses, or rewrite the sentence.
- **Do not mix scales incoherently:** a "very likely" claim built on limited, conflicting evidence is a contradiction — downgrade to a confidence statement instead.
- **Show the traceable account** in methods/discussion: what observations, models, or elicitations support each assignment, and where evidence conflicts.
- **For risk framing (AR6):** describe risk as the interaction of hazard, exposure, and vulnerability, applying calibrated language to each component rather than to an undifferentiated "risk."

## Common pitfalls

- Using likelihood words with their everyday meanings alongside calibrated uses, leaving readers unsure which register applies.
- Writing "high confidence" as if it were a probability, or "very likely" as if it were a validity judgment — the scales answer different questions.
- Assigning likelihoods where evidence is limited or disputed instead of stopping at a low-confidence statement.
- Reporting only the central estimate and dropping the range that the likelihood term summarizes.
- Overconfidence by omission: ignoring low-probability, high-consequence outcomes because they fall outside the "likely" range.
- No traceable account — calibrated tags sprinkled on conclusions with no visible link to evidence.
