## Scope

*Suggestions to Authors of the Reports of the United States Geological Survey* (7th edition, 1991; Hansen, ed.) is the Survey's comprehensive manual for preparing geoscience reports — the de facto style standard for geology, hydrology, geophysics, and related earth-science writing well beyond USGS itself.

It covers the entire publication process: planning and organizing a report, scientific writing style, geologic nomenclature and units, mathematics and statistics presentation, references, illustrations and maps, tables, and the colleague-review process. It applies to professional papers, bulletins, water-supply papers, open-file reports, journal manuscripts, and maps with accompanying pamphlets. (USGS today supplements it with online series-specific standards, but the 7th edition remains the foundational reference.)

## Key requirements

**Report planning and structure.** Plan the report before and during the investigation, not after: define audience and purpose, then outline. The standard architecture:

- Abstract — **informative, not indicative**: it states the actual findings, numbers, and conclusions, not merely what topics the report "discusses."
- Introduction — purpose and scope, location and setting, previous work, methods, acknowledgments. Every report needs a clear purpose-and-scope statement so readers know what question is answered and what is excluded.
- Body — organized by the science (stratigraphy, structure, geochemistry, hydrology — whatever the study demands).
- Interpretation/discussion, then conclusions or summary, then references.

**Writing style.** The manual's core values are accuracy, clarity, brevity, and organization:

- Plain, direct sentences; active voice where natural; concrete nouns and strong verbs.
- Distinguish rigorously between **observation and interpretation** — what was seen in the outcrop or the data versus what the author infers from it — and signal the distinction in the prose.
- Avoid stacked hedges ("may possibly suggest"), jargon where plain terms exist, and "fine writing" that decorates rather than informs.
- Use terms precisely and consistently: formal stratigraphic names capitalized per the North American Stratigraphic Code; careful "since"/"because" and "while"/"although" distinctions.

**Technical apparatus.**

- *Nomenclature and units:* follow accepted stratigraphic, mineralogic, and hydrologic nomenclature; metric units (with conversions where needed); significant figures that reflect measurement precision.
- *Numbers and mathematics:* define all symbols; present equations readably; report uncertainty honestly.
- *Illustrations and maps:* every figure and plate must earn its place, be cited in text in order, and carry complete explanations (legends), scales, and orientation; maps need credible bases, stated datums, and sources.
- *Tables:* self-explanatory, with headnotes, units in column heads, and footnotes for exceptions.
- *References:* complete and verified; cite what was actually consulted; author-date citation style.

**Review norms.** Every USGS report passes **colleague (peer) review** — typically two technical reviewers — plus editorial review and, where relevant, checks of maps, nomenclature, and data before director's approval. Authors are expected to answer every review comment, revise or rebut with reasons, and verify all data, citations, and computations themselves; review does not transfer responsibility from the author.

## How to apply when writing

- Outline before drafting; make the introduction answer purpose, scope, setting, prior work, and methods in that order.
- Write the abstract last and make it carry the findings: key numbers, key interpretations, one or two sentences of implication — a reader should get the report's substance from the abstract alone.
- In Methods, specify instruments, procedures, datums, and precision so measurements are reproducible.
- In Results, keep observation separate from inference; move interpretation to clearly labeled discussion sections.
- Use consistent formal nomenclature for units, formations, and time terms; do not coin new stratigraphic names without following the governing code.
- Number figures and tables in citation order; write captions that stand alone; put the data source and datum on every map.
- Treat colleague review as part of the science: keep a written response to every comment and reverify any number touched during revision.

## Common pitfalls

- Indicative abstracts ("this report discusses the hydrogeology of…") that omit the actual findings.
- Blurring observation and interpretation, so readers cannot tell mapped fact from inferred structure.
- Inconsistent or informal stratigraphic and unit nomenclature, and mixed unit systems without conversions.
- Figures without scales, north arrows, datums, or self-contained explanations.
- More significant figures than the measurements justify, implying false precision.
- Citing references secondhand without verifying them, or leaving review comments unanswered.
